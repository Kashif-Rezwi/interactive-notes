CLASS CORE
UNIT 1 · INVARIANT DIRECTIONS

Some vectors survive a transformation without turning

Most vectors get bent and stretched when a matrix acts on them. But every square matrix hides a few special directions that come out pointing exactly the way they went in — only their length changes. Finding those directions is the first step in almost every decomposition in this lesson.

A v = λ v
  • A: a square matrix, thought of as a transformation
  • v: the eigenvector — a nonzero direction that does not turn
  • λ: the eigenvalue — the stretch factor applied along v
  • Read it as: "applying A to v gives the same result as just scaling v by λ." That sameness is the whole point.

A first example source

Take the diagonal matrix A = diag(2, 3): it doubles the x-direction and triples the y-direction. Check the two axis vectors:

A [1,0]ᵀ = [2,0]ᵀ = 2·[1,0]ᵀ → v = [1,0] is an eigenvector, λ = 2
A [0,1]ᵀ = [0,3]ᵀ = 3·[0,1]ᵀ → v = [0,1] is an eigenvector, λ = 3

Every other direction gets skewed: for v = [1,1]ᵀ the output is [2,3]ᵀ, which no longer points along [1,1]ᵀ — the two sides of the equation grow at different rates.

FOUNDATION Two tools the notes use without naming them

The identity matrix I is the do-nothing transformation: ones on the diagonal, zeros elsewhere. Multiplying by I changes nothing, just like multiplying a number by 1.

The determinant of a 2×2 matrix [[a,b],[c,d]] is the number det = ad − bc. Geometrically it is the area scale of the transformation. It becomes exactly 0 precisely when the transformation flattens the plane onto a line — some nonzero direction gets crushed to zero.

The spectrum and the characteristic equation

The full set of eigenvalues {λ₁, λ₂, …, λₙ} is called the spectrum of the matrix. "Spectral" methods in machine learning — spectral clustering, spectral normalization, spectral graph theory — all take their name from this set. How do we find the λ values? Solve one equation:

det(A − λI) = 0
  • det: the determinant (area scale) just introduced
  • I: the identity matrix — so A − λI is "A shrunk by λ along its diagonal"
  • λ: the unknown we solve for
  • Why it makes sense: if A v = λ v for some nonzero v, then (A − λI) v = 0 — the shrunk matrix crushes a nonzero direction, which happens exactly when its determinant is 0.
  • What the result tells you: every root λ of this equation is an eigenvalue, and each comes with its own eigenvector.
A = diag(2,3) → det(A − λI) = (2−λ)(3−λ) = 0 → λ = 2 or λ = 3
The spectrum of A is {2, 3} — matching the two axis stretches we saw directly.

The geometric reading source

Eigenvectors are invariant directions: axes the transformation respects. The eigenvalue on each axis is its expansion or contraction rate. A matrix with all |λ| > 1 expands along every eigen-direction; one with a |λ| < 1 contracts along that axis; a negative λ flips the axis end-to-end while scaling it. Stable patterns in data live along directions like these — which is why the notes call eigen-analysis the foundation of PCA, spectral methods, dynamical systems, and covariance analysis. We will collect exactly those payoffs in Units 3–5.

UNIT 2 · DIAGONALIZATION

Rewriting a matrix in its own coordinates makes powers trivial

FOUNDATION Two bridges first

Matrix inverse: for a matrix P with det ≠ 0 there is an undo matrix P⁻¹ with P P⁻¹ = I. Only transformations that do not crush the plane can be undone.

Linearly independent vectors: vectors none of which can be built from the others. Two independent vectors in the plane give a genuine coordinate grid — you can describe every point by "so much of v₁ plus so much of v₂."

The idea

Eigenvectors give a matrix its own natural coordinate grid. If we stack n independent eigenvectors as the columns of P, and the matching eigenvalues on the diagonal of D, the transformation A becomes plain axis-scaling in those coordinates:

A = P D P⁻¹
  • P: eigenvector matrix — each column is one eigenvector (the new axes)
  • D: diagonal matrix of eigenvalues, in the same order as P's columns
  • P⁻¹: the inverse of P — converts standard coordinates into eigen-coordinates
  • Read it as: "to apply A, convert to eigen-coordinates (P⁻¹), scale each axis by its λ (D), convert back (P)."
Aⁿ = P Dⁿ P⁻¹
  • n: the power you want
  • Dⁿ: raise each diagonal entry to the n-th power — that is all
  • Why it makes sense: applying A n times means converting once, scaling n times, converting back once — the conversions sandwich the scalings.
  • What the result tells you: huge powers of A cost three matrix products instead of n−1 sequential multiplications.

A fully worked diagonalization constructed example

Let A = [[3, 1], [0, 2]]. The notes promise A = PDP⁻¹ makes A⁵ easy — let's hold it to that.

Characteristic equation: (3−λ)(2−λ) = 0 → λ = 3, 2
λ=3: (A−3I)v=0 → v₁ = [1, 0]ᵀ  ·  λ=2: (A−2I)v=0 → v₂ = [1, −1]ᵀ
P = [[1, 1], [0, −1]]   D = diag(3, 2)   P⁻¹ = [[1, 1], [0, −1]] (this P is its own inverse)
Check: P D P⁻¹ = [[3, 1], [0, 2]] = A ✓
A⁵ = P · diag(3⁵, 2⁵) · P⁻¹ = P · diag(243, 32) · P⁻¹ = [[243, 211], [0, 32]]
Direct check: A²=[[9,5],[0,4]], A³=[[27,19],[0,8]], A⁴=[[81,65],[0,16]], A⁵=[[243,211],[0,32]] ✓ — five diagonal multiplications instead of four full matrix products, and the gap grows with n.

When is it possible? source

A matrix is diagonalizable exactly when it has n linearly independent eigenvectors — enough of them to fill every column of P. If the eigenvectors run out, P cannot be built, and no diagonal form exists. (The notes state this as "geometric multiplicity = algebraic multiplicity"; both phrases count eigenvectors for one λ — how many independent ones exist versus how many times λ repeats as a root — see the optional panel.)

EXTENSION The multiplicity names, precisely

Algebraic multiplicity: how many times λ appears as a root of det(A−λI)=0. Geometric multiplicity: how many independent eigenvectors share that λ. A is diagonalizable iff they match for every eigenvalue. Example of failure: A = [[2,1],[0,2]] has λ=2 twice (algebraic 2) but only one independent eigenvector [1,0]ᵀ (geometric 1) — not diagonalizable, however you arrange P.

P converts eigen-coordinates to standard coordinates and P⁻¹ converts back; D scales each eigen-axis independently. When you apply the sandwich n times in a row, the interior conversions cancel in pairs — P⁻¹P = I — leaving a single P and a single P⁻¹ around Dⁿ. So Aⁿ = P Dⁿ P⁻¹, and Dⁿ is just n-th powers of the eigenvalues. The cost of "raise A to the 100th" collapses to two conversions plus 100 tiny number powers.
Common trap: diagonalization is not repeated multiplication — it is a change of coordinates. The power shortcut works because the conversions cancel in pairs, not because someone pre-multiplied A a hundred times.

UNIT 3 · SINGULAR VALUE DECOMPOSITION

Every matrix, even a rectangular one, is rotate–scale–rotate

FOUNDATION One bridge first

The transpose Aᵀ flips a matrix across its diagonal (rows become columns). For a data matrix with samples as rows, XᵀX is the "feature-agreement" matrix: entry (i, j) adds up how features i and j move together across samples.

Why SVD exists when eigen-decomposition is not enough source

Eigen decomposition demands a square matrix and enough eigenvectors. Real ML data is rarely so polite: a user–item ratings table has thousands of users (rows) and hundreds of items (columns). The notes call SVD "the most important matrix decomposition in ML" because it drops both demands — every real matrix, square or rectangular, has one.

A = U Σ Vᵀ
  • A: any real matrix — m×n, square or rectangular
  • Vᵀ: the transpose of V — whose columns (the right singular vectors) are the input directions A respects
  • Σ: a diagonal-shaped table of singular values σ₁ ≥ σ₂ ≥ … ≥ 0 — nonnegative, always sorted largest first
  • U: whose columns (the left singular vectors) are the output directions those inputs land on
  • Read it as: "take an input direction from V, scale it by its σ, and it emerges along the matching direction in U."

The geometric reading: three stages source

Any linear transformation is secretly three simple moves:

Step 1 — a rotation/reflection (Vᵀ) lines the input directions up with the axes. Step 2 — axis-wise scaling (Σ) stretches each axis by its singular value. Step 3 — another rotation/reflection (U) points the stretched axes at the output directions. A unit circle therefore always maps to an ellipse whose semi-axes are the σᵢ — that is why "every linear transformation can be interpreted geometrically."

What the σᵢ mean source

A singular value measures the strength of one direction pair: how much of A's action flows from input direction vᵢ to output direction uᵢ. Larger σ → more of the transformation's "energy" travels that route. The notes add: smaller values are "likely noise" — the careful version is that they can carry less dominant structure, or noise; which one depends on the data, and Unit 5 shows how to measure the tradeoff instead of guessing.

The eigenvalue link source

You already own the tool for finding σ: Unit 1's eigenvalues, applied to AᵀA.

σᵢ = √( λᵢ(AᵀA) )
  • σᵢ: the i-th singular value of A
  • λᵢ(AᵀA): the i-th eigenvalue of AᵀA (always nonnegative)
  • √: square root
  • Why it makes sense: AᵀA measures how A scales each input direction in total; the square undoes the "scale twice" effect.
  • What the result tells you: compute eigenvalues of AᵀA, take square roots, sort descending — those are the singular values. The eigenvectors of AᵀA are the vᵢ.

A complete numeric SVD, worked constructed example

Let A = [[2, 1], [1, 2]] — small enough to check by hand. First AᵀA:

AᵀA = [[2,1],[1,2]]·[[2,1],[1,2]] = [[5, 4], [4, 5]]
det(AᵀA − λI) = (5−λ)² − 16 = 0 → λ² − 10λ + 9 = 0 → λ = 9 or 1
λ=9 → v₁ = [1, 1]ᵀ/√2  ·  λ=1 → v₂ = [1, −1]ᵀ/√2
σ₁ = √9 = 3, σ₂ = √1 = 1
u₁ = A v₁ / σ₁ = [3, 3]ᵀ/(√2·3) = [1, 1]ᵀ/√2  ·  u₂ = A v₂ / σ₂ = [1, −1]ᵀ/√2
U = V = [[1/√2, 1/√2], [1/√2, −1/√2]]   Σ = diag(3, 1)
Verify: U Σ Vᵀ = [[1/√2,1/√2],[1/√2,−1/√2]]·diag(3,1)·[[1/√2,1/√2],[1/√2,−1/√2]]ᵀ = [[2,1],[1,2]] = A ✓

The picture: A takes the diagonal direction [1,1]/√2 and stretches it to length 3; the anti-diagonal direction keeps length 1. The unit circle becomes an ellipse with semi-axes 3 and 1 — which is exactly what the explorer below draws, computing all of these numbers live.

UNIT 4 · PRINCIPAL COMPONENT ANALYSIS

The data's own eigen-directions are the directions that matter

FOUNDATION Three bridges first

Variance is the average squared deviation from the mean — the spread of a feature. Mean-centering subtracts each feature's mean from every sample, so the data cloud sits on the origin and only its shape remains. Projection drops a point onto a direction, keeping "how far along" and discarding "how far off."

What PCA wants source

High-dimensional data is often redundant: many features move together. PCA finds the directions along which the data varies most (maximum variance) and which are mutually uncorrelated (minimum redundancy), then projects onto the top few — compressing many features into a few "principal components" without a linear-algebra fuss per feature.

The covariance matrix

C = (1/n) Xᵀ X
  • X: the mean-centered data matrix — one row per sample, one column per feature
  • n: number of samples
  • C: the covariance matrix — entry (i, j) is the covariance (shared spread) of features i and j; the diagonal is each feature's variance
  • Why it makes sense: XᵀX adds up, feature-pair by feature-pair, how the centered samples move together; dividing by n makes it an average.
  • What the result tells you: C is symmetric and nonnegative — exactly the kind of matrix whose eigen-directions are real, orthogonal, and meaningful.

The payoff: PCA is SVD on centered data source

Here is where Units 1 and 3 cash in. PCA computes the eigenvectors of C and keeps the largest eigenvalues. But for centered X, the matrix C = (1/n)XᵀX has exactly the eigenvectors as the SVD's V — because the vᵢ are the eigen-directions of XᵀX. So:

X = U Σ Vᵀ  (centered X)  ⇒  columns of V = principal directions
  • V: right singular vectors — one column per principal component
  • U Σ: the scores — where each sample lands on each component
  • Read it as: "run SVD on centered data and you have done PCA; no separate eigen-solver needed."
varᵢ = σᵢ² / n
  • σᵢ: the i-th singular value of the centered X
  • n: number of samples
  • varᵢ: the variance captured by principal component i
  • Why it makes sense: C = (1/n)XᵀX has eigenvalues λᵢ = σᵢ²/n — the eigenvalue is the variance along vᵢ, and EQ-006's square root links σ back.
  • What the result tells you: to compare components, compare σᵢ² — the "energy" bookkeeping that Unit 5 reuses for compression.

The notes state the ordering rule: principal components are the covariance eigenvectors sorted by descending eigenvalue — equivalently, the V columns in SVD's built-in descending-σ order. No extra sort needed.

The workflow, end to end source

Step 1: center the data (subtract feature means). Step 2: form the covariance matrix C = (1/n)XᵀX. Step 3: eigenvectors of C — or, identically, the SVD of X. Step 4: keep the top-k components. Step 5: project the data onto them and work in the new, smaller coordinates.

3. Explain in your own words. Why is "PCA = SVD" true only for centered data? Compose your answer, then compare with the model.

PCA is defined by the covariance matrix C = (1/n)XᵀX, whose eigen-directions are the spread directions of the centered cloud. The SVD's V is the eigen-directions of XᵀX. Those two eigen-problems coincide only when XᵀX is (a constant times) the covariance — which is exactly what centering guarantees, because a centered cloud has its mean at the origin and XᵀX measures pure spread. Skip centering and XᵀX mixes spread with the cloud's offset: the first singular direction points toward the mean instead of along the widest spread, so the SVD no longer solves PCA's problem.
Common trap: PCA does not maximize the mean or the range — it maximizes variance after centering. A feature with huge values but no spread contributes nothing to PC1.
CONNECT · WHERE PCA SHOWS UP ML LINK

The notes list the real uses: computer vision — face recognition ("eigenfaces" are literally principal components of face images); NLP — shrinking word embeddings before downstream training; finance — risk factor analysis (a few principal components of asset returns summarize market-wide risk); bioinformatics — compressing gene-expression matrices. In every case the mechanism is the same one you just drove: center, decompose, keep the top directions, project.

UNIT 5 · LOW-RANK APPROXIMATION

Keep the strongest components, drop the rest, keep most of the meaning

FOUNDATION One bridge first

Rank is the number of independent directions a matrix genuinely uses — equivalently, the number of nonzero singular values. A rank-1 matrix is pure "one input direction → one output direction"; a full-rank matrix uses every direction it has.

The move source

SVD sorts a matrix's structure by importance. Low-rank approximation exploits that ordering: keep only the top k singular values and their directions, discard the rest.

A_k = U_k Σ_k V_kᵀ
  • U_k: only the first k columns of U
  • Σ_k: only the k largest singular values
  • V_kᵀ: only the first k rows of Vᵀ
  • Read it as: "rebuild the matrix from its k strongest direction pairs only."
  • Why it makes sense: real data has redundancy, hidden structure, correlated features — a few strong directions carry most of the content, so a small k often reconstructs almost everything.
retained energy = ( σ₁² + … + σ_k² ) / ( σ₁² + … + σ_r² )
  • σᵢ²: squared singular values — the "energy" a direction pair carries (variance × n, per Unit 4)
  • k: how many components you keep; r: the rank (all nonzero components)
  • What the result tells you: the share of the matrix's total energy your k components explain. The same bookkeeping as PCA's retained variance — deliberately the same.
‖ A − A_k ‖₂ = σ_{k+1}
  • ‖·‖₂: the spectral norm — the largest singular value of the remainder
  • σ_{k+1}: the first singular value you discarded
  • Why it makes sense: the remainder A − A_k is itself a matrix whose singular values are exactly the discarded ones.
  • What the result tells you: the worst-direction error of the compression is precisely the biggest thing you threw away — you can price the truncation before you commit.

The notes' error claim becomes concrete: "lower discarded singular values → better approximation" is exactly σ_{k+1} shrinking as k grows. And the earlier "likely noise" hunch now has a measurement: if the discarded σᵢ contribute a percent of energy, their loss is small whether they were noise or weak structure — the energy budget tells you the cost either way.

DEEP DIVE Latent spaces — the closing idea source

Modern AI leans on one structural bet: high-dimensional observations are generated by a few hidden causes. A ratings table is generated by a few tastes; an image by a few shapes and lighting factors; a sentence by a few meanings. Low-rank structure is that bet made numerical — the rank-k columns of U are the latent space, coordinates in which the data becomes simple. The notes close with this as one of the deepest ideas in generative AI, representation learning, and world models: diffusion models generate in latent spaces; representation learning is the art of finding them; a world model is a latent space with dynamics. You now know the linear-algebra seed of that idea: find the strongest direction pairs, and complexity collapses.

GLOSSARY · EVERY TERM, SIX WAYS

Glossary

eigenvector
Simple: a direction a matrix does not turn. Precise: a nonzero vector v with A v = λ v for some scalar λ. Intuition: the transformation's own preferred axes. Example: [1,0]ᵀ for diag(2,3). Related: eigenvalue, spectrum, diagonalization. In practice/ML: principal components, spectral clustering, stability analysis.
eigenvalue
Simple: how far the eigenvector's direction stretches. Precise: the scalar λ satisfying A v = λ v. Intuition: expansion/contraction rate along an invariant axis. Example: λ = 2 and 3 for diag(2,3). Related: eigenvector, spectrum, singular value. In practice/ML: variance per principal component; growth rates in dynamics.
spectrum
Simple: the matrix's complete set of eigenvalues. Precise: {λ₁,…,λₙ}, the roots of det(A−λI)=0. Intuition: the full stretching fingerprint of the transformation. Example: {2,3} for diag(2,3). Related: characteristic equation, eigenvalue. In practice/ML: "spectral" methods read structure off this set.
spectral methods (e.g. spectral clustering)
Simple: techniques that analyze a matrix's eigenvalues/eigenvectors. Precise: algorithms whose core step is an eigen decomposition of a similarity or graph matrix. Intuition: clusters and communities show up as separate eigen-directions. Example: clustering a similarity graph via its top eigenvectors. Related: spectrum, SVD, PCA. In practice/ML: spectral clustering, spectral normalization in GANs, spectral graph theory.
characteristic equation
Simple: the equation whose solutions are the eigenvalues. Precise: det(A − λI) = 0. Intuition: ask when "A shrunk by λ" crushes a nonzero direction. Example: (2−λ)(3−λ)=0 → λ∈{2,3}. Related: determinant, identity matrix, spectrum. In practice/ML: hand-solving small eigen problems; theory of diagonalizability.
determinant
Simple: how much the transformation scales area (2×2). Precise: for [[a,b],[c,d]], det = ad − bc; 0 exactly when some nonzero direction is crushed. Intuition: area ruler; zero means flattening. Example: det(diag(2,3)) = 6. Related: identity matrix, matrix inverse, characteristic equation. In practice/ML: invertibility tests; eigenvalue derivation.
identity matrix
Simple: the do-nothing matrix. Precise: I has 1 on the diagonal, 0 elsewhere; A I = I A = A. Intuition: the matrix version of the number 1. Example: I₂ = [[1,0],[0,1]]. Related: matrix inverse, characteristic equation. In practice/ML: reference point in the characteristic equation; default weight initialization ideas.
diagonal matrix
Simple: nonzero entries only on the diagonal. Precise: a_ij = 0 for i ≠ j; scales each axis independently. Intuition: pure per-axis stretching, no mixing. Example: diag(2,3). Related: diagonalization, eigenvalue. In practice/ML: the target form of diagonalization; Dⁿ is entry-wise.
diagonalization
Simple: rewriting a matrix in its own coordinates. Precise: A = P D P⁻¹ with P's columns independent eigenvectors and D diagonal of eigenvalues. Intuition: change coordinates so the transformation is pure axis-scaling. Example: A=[[3,1],[0,2]] = P·diag(3,2)·P⁻¹. Related: matrix inverse, linearly independent, matrix power. In practice/ML: cheap matrix powers/exponentials; covariance analysis; stability.
matrix inverse
Simple: the matrix that undoes another. Precise: P⁻¹ with P P⁻¹ = I; exists iff det(P) ≠ 0. Intuition: the reverse gear of a transformation. Example: [[1,1],[0,−1]] is its own inverse. Related: determinant, identity matrix. In practice/ML: solving linear systems; the "convert back" step of PDP⁻¹.
linearly independent
Simple: vectors none of which can be built from the others. Precise: no nontrivial combination c₁v₁+…+cₖvₖ equals zero. Intuition: each adds a genuinely new direction. Example: [1,0]ᵀ and [1,−1]ᵀ in the plane. Related: rank, diagonalization. In practice/ML: diagonalizability requires n independent eigenvectors.
matrix power
Simple: multiplying a matrix by itself repeatedly. Precise: Aⁿ = A·A·…·A (n factors). Intuition: applying the same transformation n times. Example: A⁵ = [[243,211],[0,32]] for A=[[3,1],[0,2]]. Related: diagonalization. In practice/ML: Markov chains, dynamical systems, graph walks — all made cheap by PDP⁻¹.
transpose
Simple: flip the matrix across its diagonal. Precise: (Aᵀ)ᵢⱼ = Aⱼᵢ. Intuition: rows become columns; AᵀA aggregates feature agreements. Example: [[2,1],[1,2]]ᵀ = [[2,1],[1,2]]. Related: covariance matrix, SVD. In practice/ML: XᵀX in covariance; Vᵀ in SVD.
singular value
Simple: the strength of one input→output direction pair. Precise: σᵢ = √λᵢ(AᵀA), nonnegative, sorted descending. Intuition: how much transformation energy travels that route. Example: σ = (3,1) for [[2,1],[1,2]]. Related: singular vectors, eigenvalue, rank. In practice/ML: energy budgets for PCA and compression; spectral normalization.
left / right singular vectors
Simple: output and input directions of the SVD. Precise: columns of U (left, output) and V (right, input), orthonormal sets. Intuition: matched direction pairs joined by a σ. Example: u₁ = v₁ = [1,1]ᵀ/√2 for [[2,1],[1,2]]. Related: SVD, singular value. In practice/ML: V = principal directions; U = scores/latent factors.
singular value decomposition (SVD)
Simple: every matrix is rotate–scale–rotate. Precise: A = UΣVᵀ for any real m×n A, with σ₁ ≥ σ₂ ≥ … ≥ 0. Intuition: the transformation's unit circle image is an ellipse with semi-axes σᵢ. Example: [[2,1],[1,2]] = U·diag(3,1)·Vᵀ. Related: singular value, PCA, low-rank approximation. In practice/ML: the workhorse behind PCA, recommenders, compression, denoising.
eigen decomposition
Simple: factoring a square matrix via its eigenvectors. Precise: A = PDP⁻¹ when n independent eigenvectors exist; not possible for every square matrix. Intuition: diagonalization proper. Example: the Unit 2 worked example. Related: diagonalization, SVD. In practice/ML: square symmetric matrices (covariances, graphs); SVD covers the rest.
covariance matrix
Simple: the pairwise spread table of centered data. Precise: C = (1/n)XᵀX for centered X; cᵢⱼ = covariance of features i and j. Intuition: which features move together and how much. Example: C = [[1.79,0.92],[0.92,0.98]] for the Unit 4 dataset. Related: mean-centering, variance, PCA. In practice/ML: the matrix PCA decomposes; whitening; risk models.
mean-centering
Simple: subtract each feature's average from every sample. Precise: X ← X − 1·mean(X)ᵀ per feature column. Intuition: move the cloud onto the origin; keep only shape. Example: PCA workflow Step 1. Related: covariance matrix, variance. In practice/ML: the non-negotiable first step of PCA.
variance
Simple: average squared spread around the mean. Precise: var(x) = (1/n)Σ(xᵢ − x̄)². Intuition: how far the cloud stretches. Example: diagonal entries of C. Related: covariance matrix, mean-centering. In practice/ML: what PCA maximizes per component.
principal component
Simple: a highest-variance direction of the centered data. Precise: eigenvector of C (equivalently right singular vector of centered X), ordered by descending eigenvalue. Intuition: the data's own most informative axes. Example: PC1 of the Unit 4 scatter at ≈33°. Related: PCA, eigenvalue, singular value. In practice/ML: feature compression, visualization, decorrelation.
principal component analysis (PCA)
Simple: keep only the directions where data varies most. Precise: project centered data onto the top-k eigenvectors of C; retained variance = Σᵢ≤kλᵢ/Σλ. Intuition: SVD on centered data. Example: one component kept ≈86% of the Unit 4 dataset's variance. Related: principal component, covariance matrix, SVD. In practice/ML: dimensionality reduction for vision, NLP, finance, bioinformatics.
projection
Simple: dropping a point onto a direction. Precise: the component of x along unit vector v is (x·v)v. Intuition: keep "how far along," discard "how far off." Example: PCA workflow Step 5. Related: dot product, principal component. In practice/ML: the compression step of PCA.
rank
Simple: how many independent directions a matrix really uses. Precise: the number of nonzero singular values (equivalently independent rows/columns). Intuition: the true dimensionality of the transformation's action. Example: the ratings matrix has rank 3 (all σᵢ > 0). Related: linearly independent, singular value, low-rank approximation. In practice/ML: effective model complexity; rank-k budgets.
low-rank approximation
Simple: rebuild a matrix from only its strongest components. Precise: A_k = U_kΣ_kV_kᵀ, the best rank-k matrix in spectral norm; error ‖A−A_k‖₂ = σ_{k+1}. Intuition: keep the direction pairs that carry the energy. Example: rank-1 kept ≈85% of the ratings matrix's energy. Related: SVD, rank, retained energy. In practice/ML: compression, denoising, recommenders, LoRA.
matrix factorization
Simple: writing a matrix as a product of factor matrices. Precise: A ≈ BC (or more factors); SVD and R ≈ UVᵀ are instances. Intuition: split a big table into small, meaningful pieces. Example: R ≈ U Vᵀ in recommenders. Related: SVD, low-rank approximation. In practice/ML: recommender backends; topic models; embeddings.
denoising
Simple: removing weak components that carry little structure. Precise: discard small-σ components so the rebuild keeps dominant structure (whether the discarded part was noise or weak structure is data-dependent). Intuition: noise rarely aligns with the strongest directions. Example: truncated-SVD image cleaning. Related: low-rank approximation, singular value. In practice/ML: image denoising, signal cleanup, robust pipelines.
latent factor
Simple: a hidden cause behind observed data. Precise: an unobserved variable whose combination (approximately) generates observed features — the columns of U_k/V_k in factorizations. Intuition: tastes you never asked about, recovered from ratings. Example: a "sci-fi affinity" factor in the movie matrix. Related: latent space, low-rank approximation. In practice/ML: recommender preferences, topic models, embeddings.
latent space
Simple: the coordinate system of hidden factors. Precise: a lower-dimensional representation space in which data varies simply. Intuition: coordinates where complexity collapses. Example: rank-k columns of U for the ratings matrix. Related: latent factor, low-rank approximation. In practice/ML: generative models, representation learning, world models.
recommender system
Simple: software that predicts what you will like. Precise: models filling missing entries of a user–item matrix, often via factorization R ≈ UVᵀ. Intuition: few tastes explain many ratings. Example: Netflix/Amazon/Spotify suggestions. Related: matrix factorization, latent factor. In practice/ML: collaborative filtering at industrial scale.
LoRA (low-rank adaptation)
Simple: fine-tune a huge model by learning only small low-rank updates. Precise: constrain the weight change ΔW = BA with rank-k B, A, training far fewer parameters than W. Intuition: the change a task requires is low-rank even when the model is not. Example: adapters for LLM fine-tuning. Related: low-rank approximation, transformer. In practice/ML: efficient LLM fine-tuning and serving.
transformer
Simple: the attention-based architecture behind modern language models. Precise: a neural architecture stacking self-attention and feed-forward blocks over token embeddings. Intuition: every token can look at every other token. Example: the model family behind GPT-style LLMs. Related: LoRA, latent space. In practice/ML: LLMs whose weights and attention matrices are compressed via low-rank methods.
latent semantic analysis (LSA)
Simple: find hidden topics by decomposing a word–document table. Precise: truncated SVD of a term–document matrix; top vᵢ are topics. Intuition: documents that use similar words land close in topic space. Example: classic information retrieval. Related: SVD, latent factor. In practice/ML: search, topic discovery, embeddings before neural methods.
diffusion model
Simple: image (and more) generators that learn to reverse a noising process. Precise: a network trained to denoise step by step, usually operating in a compressed latent space. Intuition: sculpting order out of noise. Example: modern text-to-image systems. Related: latent space, denoising. In practice/ML: generative AI built on low-dimensional latent representations.
